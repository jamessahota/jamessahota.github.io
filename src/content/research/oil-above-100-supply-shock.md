keyFindings:
  - >-
    Physical tightness came first. Dated Brent traded above US$100 from 3 September, before the
    surge in front-month futures, which supported the view that prompt scarcity was real and
    not only a reaction to military headlines.
  - >-
    Spare capacity sat on the wrong side of the chokepoint. The IEA estimated only 1.09 mb/d of
    effective OPEC+ spare capacity in July. Bypass pipelines offered a potential 3.5–5.5 mb/d,
    and the IEA warned that the upper end had not been robustly tested.
  - >-
    Refined products were tighter than crude. Diesel and jet fuel exports from Russia, the
    Middle East and Asia were sharply lower, and the report said product tightness could persist
    even if Brent fell on a ceasefire.
  - >-
    Backwardation was the market’s working assumption. An ICE snapshot on 9 September showed
    Brent at US$100.45 for November 2026 and US$77.87 for December 2027. The curve priced
    scarcity now and relief later.
  - >-
    Deferred prices were the test of permanence. The view would change if insured flows through
    Hormuz recovered for two consecutive weeks or, in the other direction, if Gulf output stayed
    at least 4 mb/d below pre-war levels into 2027 while deferred Brent moved above US$100.

The report’s starting point is that the move above US$100 was not a purely speculative war premium. By early September the EIA estimated that 6.7 million barrels a day of Gulf crude production had been shut in during August, and the IEA estimated that observed global inventories had fallen by 410 million barrels between the start of the war and the end of July. Dated Brent, the physical benchmark, had traded above US$100 before front-month futures followed.

The report then argues that a price above US$100 already discounted a serious and persistent disruption. The futures curve fell sharply into 2027, demand was weakening under high prices, and pipelines, ship-to-ship transfers and strategic stocks were absorbing part of the shock.

My base case was Brent at US$95–115 over the following month, US$85–100 at the end of 2026 and an annual average of US$70–90 in 2027. I held that view with medium confidence, because dark shipping obscured true flows and a single diplomatic or military event could move the front of the curve by double digits.
